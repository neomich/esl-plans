“Americans Don’t Use Present Perfect!”: A Shitty Reason to Stop Learning Grammar

4 November 2026 · by Alex Selivanov · ESL-plans Teacher's Corner

“Americans don’t use that grammar” is a weak excuse to stop learning it. Native speakers make different choices depending on the person, situation and purpose. Learning English grammar gives you those choices too. Skip it, and you’re not keeping things simple by choice. You’re working with whatever you’ve got.

My American friend said…

I check out forums and social media quite often. Between the arguments and unsolicited advice, I regularly come across this little gem: “My American friend told me nobody uses the present perfect continuous or future perfect. So fuck it. Don’t bother.” Conditionals? Apparently, one structure will do for any situation. People will understand. Relax. It’s not worth the effort. Lovely. Years of language teaching sorted out by someone’s mate.

I get why this advice is popular. Grammar takes work. Someone tells you that you can skip the difficult bits and still speak English? Excellent news. Close the book. We’re done here.

Except we’re not. “Somebody I know doesn’t use it” is not the same as “you don’t need to learn it.”

Which Americans, though?

“Americans don’t use it.” Which Americans? Your colleague? A university lecturer? A teenager on TikTok? The guys hanging around Skid Row? These guys are proud Americans, yet they hardly wipe their asses. Would that be a good reason to lower your standards of hygiene?
That’s how convincing I find the argument. You can admire someone’s humour, confidence or ability to tell a story without treating every feature of their speech as a learning objective.

Also, knowing a language and knowing how to explain it are different skills. Ask a native speaker whether they use the present perfect continuous. You might get a blank stare. Ask them how long they’ve been working there.

“I’ve been working here for six years.”

Well, would you look at that. Not knowing the name of a structure doesn’t mean you don’t use it. And not noticing it in conversation doesn’t mean it isn’t there.

People will understand. Then what?

Yes, people can often understand imperfect English. “Yesterday I go office. Boss angry. Report not ready.”

The basic story survives. So, unfortunately, does the boss.
For a beginner, getting that message across is progress. Speak. Make mistakes. Nobody needs to hold a grammar tribunal every time you open your mouth. But if you’ve been learning English for years, is “they sort of understood me” really where you want to stop?
People perceive you through more than your clothes and haircut. They also notice how you express yourself. How clearly you explain a problem. How you disagree. Whether your request sounds polite, hesitant or like an order.

That doesn’t make every judgement fair. An accent is not a measure of intelligence. Neither is a misplaced article. Still, your language affects how you’re heard.

“Send me the report.”

“Could you send me the report?”

“I was wondering if you could send me the report.”

Same report. Different impression. Potentially a different reply, too. You should get to choose which one you mean.

Simple by choice - or by necessity?

The language you use is personal. It’s how you speak and how you wish to be heard. Want to keep it casual? Fine. Prefer short sentences? Great. Want to swear? Pick your audience. But if you haven’t learned the alternatives, you don’t have the same freedom.

Compare:

“I worked on your proposal.”

“I’ve been working on your proposal.”

The first reports past work. The second brings that activity into the present conversation: perhaps it’s still going on, or perhaps you’re explaining what has been taking up your time.

Now try these:

“If you send it today, I’ll check it.”

“If you’d sent it yesterday, I would have checked it.”

One offers help. The other explains a missed opportunity.

“Just use one conditional for everything” suddenly looks less helpful when you need to tell a client that yesterday’s deadline was, in fact, yesterday.

Could you explain the difference with simpler language? Often, yes. But having to walk around every structure you haven’t learned gets tiring. Sometimes the supposedly difficult grammar is the shortest route to what you mean.

Yes, I’m actually using it

If you have been studying English for years, you’ve probably met these structures before. If you had skipped them completely, some of the meaning here might have escaped you. By the time you finish this article, you will have seen them doing perfectly ordinary work. There you go. Present perfect continuous. A conditional. Future perfect.

No academic conference. No powdered wig. Just a teacher having a rant on the internet.

I’m not arguing that you should cram complicated structures into every sentence. That would sound ridiculous.

“I shall have consumed my sandwich by the time the meeting commences.”

Mate. Eat your fucking lunch and get back to your messy desk!

The point is to use a structure when it helps - not to perform grammatical gymnastics so everyone knows you passed C1.

But don’t confuse “keep it simple” with “complex structures don’t belong in everyday English.” They do. They give you more ways to express yourself. Grammar, vocabulary, sentence structure - you name it. Most importantly, they give you room to develop your own style.

Because guess what? People may forget your shoes or which handbag you were carrying. How you speak can stick around much longer.

You might make a grammar mistake or two. Fine. If you’re funny, engaging or have something worth saying, a missing article won’t erase the impression you’ve made. When an opportunity comes up - or you need a callback or a favour - you want to be someone people remember, not someone they vaguely recall meeting somewhere.

That’s not about speaking perfect English. It’s about having enough control over the language to let your personality come through.

Your language style is part of what makes you YOU. Why settle for fewer ways to show it?

Give the Grammar a Job

This is where teachers have some responsibility, too. If our only explanation is “you need this for the exam,” we shouldn’t be shocked when students ask whether they can skip it. Show them what it does.

Present perfect continuous? Talk about a problem that has been driving them mad lately. Their neighbour’s renovation. Job hunting. Trying to cancel a subscription that apparently requires a blood sacrifice.

Conditionals? Discuss a decision they regret, a risk they’re considering or advice they wish they’d ignored.

Future perfect? Ask what they expect to have finished by Friday - and which deadline is already looking optimistic.

Now there’s a reason to use the form. It helps them say something they actually mean. And no, grammar isn’t the only thing worth learning. Listening, vocabulary, pronunciation and confidence matter too. This isn’t a competition. But “don’t obsess over every mistake” is sensible advice.

“Don’t bother learning the difference” is something else entirely.

Learn the options. Practise them. Then decide what suits your voice. Keep your slang. Keep your humour. Call shit shit. But why let someone else’s limitations become the ceiling for your English? Of your style? Of YOU?
