Textbook Fatigue Is Real. And It’s Not Just a Student Problem

21 October 2026 · ESL-plans Teacher's Corner

Textbook fatigue happens when safe, generic materials replace meaningful communication. Teenagers and adults rarely connect with the same topics, and teachers get bored too. The fix is not another magic activity: listen to what students care about, then use their lives, opinions and frustrations as the starting point for better lessons.

Let’s be honest: plenty of ESL textbooks are trying to serve teenagers and adults at the same time.

Newsflash: adults are not teenagers. And teenagers hardly ever look up to adults as their natural source of inspiration.

So how do publishers satisfy both groups simultaneously? Usually, with compromise: a safe, watered-down approach to materials design and delivery that does not really fit either group. The textbook checks the boxes for the management upstairs, looks neutral enough to be sold globally, and avoids upsetting anyone.

But the classroom is left wanting something more. Something real. Something relatable.

This is textbook fatigue.

It is not simply students getting bored halfway through Unit 6. It is that familiar feeling when you open a coursebook and already know what is coming: another polished photo, another generic discussion question, another text about a perfectly harmless topic nobody has strong feelings about.

Students feel it. Teachers feel it too.

The irony is that textbooks are not inherently bad. They offer structure, progression and a ready-made sequence of grammar and vocabulary. That matters, especially for less experienced teachers or groups that need a clear framework.

The problem begins when the book becomes the lesson instead of supporting it.

The Great Compromise

A lot of mainstream materials are designed for a broad age range: older teens, university students and adults. On paper, that sounds efficient. In reality, it often produces lessons that feel strangely disconnected from everyone in the room.

Teenagers may find it hard to get excited about yet another text on global warming, space exploration or a fictional adult changing careers. They are not stupid; they know these topics matter. But “important” does not automatically mean “interesting.” If a topic has no emotional connection to their lives, they will simply go through the motions.

Adults, meanwhile, may politely smile at another Shakespeare-focused reading, Halloween worksheet or role-play where they pretend to be exchange students planning a weekend trip. Again, these activities are not always useless. But adults bring actual experience into the room: jobs, relationships, disappointments, ambitions, family pressure, money worries, technology, changing habits and strong opinions.

They do not need to pretend they have a life outside the classroom. They already have one.

That is why a discussion question such as “Would you like to travel to Mars?” can sometimes fall completely flat, while “Would you stop working if AI made money irrelevant?” can get a quiet B1 student talking for fifteen minutes.

That is the difference between a topic that fills space and a topic that creates a conversation.

My Work Is Optional lesson https://esl-plans.com/#lesson-purpose, for example, uses the idea of a future where work and money may become optional. It is not “business English” in the dull corporate sense. It gets learners discussing identity, purpose, technology and what they would actually do with their lives if they did not have to earn a living. The lesson is built for adult B1–B2 learners and combines video, audio, language work and discussion rather than treating the topic as a decorative warm-up.

Why Teachers Get Tired Too

Textbook fatigue is not only about students being bored. Tutors get tired of teaching the same artificial scenarios as well.

After a while, it becomes difficult to sound genuinely curious when you have asked “What are the advantages of living in the countryside?” for the tenth time that year. You know the expected answers. Your student knows the expected answers. Everybody is performing a conversation rather than having one.

And in one-to-one classes, this becomes even more obvious. There is nowhere to hide. If the learner is not interested, the energy drops immediately.

Teachers also get stuck because textbooks create a false sense of safety. The unit is there. The exercises are numbered. The audio is ready. There is a grammar box on page 74. Great.

But a neat page is not the same thing as a good lesson.

A good lesson gives students a reason to use English beyond completing Exercise 3B. It allows them to react, disagree, make connections and occasionally go off-script. That is where the language becomes memorable.

This does not mean every lesson needs to be provocative, political or painfully personal. It simply means the material should give people something worth reacting to.

A topic like asynchronous work can do exactly that https://esl-plans.com/#lesson-asynchronous-work. Instead of drilling workplace vocabulary through another meeting role-play, learners can compare remote and office routines, discuss productivity, talk about digital habits and reflect on how work has changed in their own lives. The lesson also creates a natural context for language such as used to, tend to and get used to.

Stop Asking Reddit for a Magic Activity

I occasionally enter the toxic forest of Reddit. Some posts end up inspiring my lesson plans, by the way.

But one type of post appears again and again: “My students are disengaged. Please suggest an activity!”

The replies are usually predictable: use a game, try Kahoot, bring snacks, make them move around the room, show a funny video, use a debate.

None of these things is necessarily bad. But they are not a cure.

If learners are disengaged, perhaps the first question should not be, “Which activity will wake them up?” Perhaps it should be, “What is going on in their lives, and what do they actually care about?”

Ask your students.

Not necessarily in a formal “Please complete this needs-analysis questionnaire” way. You can do it indirectly. Ask them what they have been watching, what annoys them at work, which apps they hate but cannot stop using, what changed after they moved country, what social trend they do not understand, or what they would ban if they had the power.

Listen carefully. Their answers are lesson material.

A student complaining about being constantly distracted by their phone can lead to a lesson on algorithms. A learner frustrated with workplace meetings can lead to a class about asynchronous work. Someone mentioning online scams, social media pressure or AI dating apps can open up a much richer conversation than “Describe your favorite season.”

The point is not to become your students’ therapist. The point is to notice what already carries emotional weight for them.

My Algorithms https://esl-plans.com/#lesson-algorithms lesson takes a topic students encounter every day but may not have fully examined: how platforms shape what they see, buy and believe. It moves from a simple explanation of algorithms to conversations about convenience, manipulation and whether people should trust AI with personal decisions.

How to Manage Textbook Fatigue

You do not have to throw your coursebook into the bin and become a full-time materials creator. Use it as a skeleton if it helps. Just do not let it control the whole body.

A few practical ways to break the routine:

  • Keep the language aim, change the context. If the book teaches past habits, use your student’s changing routines rather than a made-up character’s lifestyle.

  • Replace one task, not the whole lesson. Swap the bland reading or discussion stage for a short video, an article, a Reddit post, a podcast clip or a real-life dilemma.

  • Build a topic bank for each student. Keep notes on interests, work issues, hobbies, opinions and recurring complaints. This is gold for future lessons.

  • Use student language as material. Their mistakes, stories and questions are more relevant than many textbook dialogues.

  • Let students disagree. A lesson does not need unanimous, polite answers. Respectful disagreement produces real language.

  • Rotate the energy. Not every class needs deep discussion. Mix serious topics with strange, funny or nostalgic ones. A lesson on one-hit wonders, for instance, can be a lighter way to discuss fame, music and cultural memory while still doing meaningful language work.

  • Give yourself permission to skip. You paid for the book; it does not mean every page deserves classroom time.

  • Remember! The textbooks are designed and compiled by English tutors just like you. You may disagree with where they take you and offer your perspective - because you have one, don't you?

Textbook fatigue happens when materials become predictable, detached and overly safe. Students are not empty containers waiting to receive the next grammar point. Adults especially arrive with opinions, stories and problems worth talking about.

Use that.

The best lessons are not always the slickest ones. Sometimes they begin with a student saying, “You know what really annoys me?” and the teacher being smart enough to follow the thread.

That is not a lack of planning.

That is good teaching.
