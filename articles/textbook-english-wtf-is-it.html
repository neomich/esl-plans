Textbook English - WTF Is It?

22 November 2026 · by Alex · ESL-plans Teacher's Corner

“Authentic English for real-life situations.”

You see it in tutors’ profiles, course descriptions and lesson ads. Then you open the material, and everyone is politely discussing their career goals. Nobody gets pissed off. Nobody makes a dirty joke. Even the office asshole expresses his concerns respectfully.

After class, we recommend a podcast or a film.

And suddenly, our students are dealing with a rather different English. By themselves.

That’s the bit that bothers me. We encourage adults to explore the language outside the classroom, but some of us get suspiciously attached to risk-free material when they bring that language back.

Go watch a film. No, don’t ask about that.

Goodfellas contains around 300 uses of “fuck.” The Wolf of Wall Street has over 500!

Yet an adult student can take English lessons for years without having a proper conversation about what the word does. The Hollywood classics aren’t authentic enough? WTF?

Of course, a Scorsese film isn’t a model for chatting with your colleagues. That’s precisely why some explanation might be useful.

Which expressions are openly aggressive? Which are jokes between friends? What should students understand but probably avoid saying themselves?

“Watch more films” is easy advice. Being available for the questions afterwards requires a little more work.

Whose discomfort are we avoiding?

Commercial textbooks give us structure and save preparation time. I have used prepared material too. There’s nothing inherently wrong with a polite dialogue about a delayed delivery.

But a textbook also gives the teacher somewhere to hide.

“This isn’t part of today’s lesson.”

“You don’t need that expression.”

“Let’s stick to proper English.”

Sometimes leaving something out is a reasonable teaching decision. Sometimes we just don’t fancy explaining a sexual reference at ten in the morning. Those aren’t the same thing though. 

I’m talking about adult learners here. We don’t need to spring explicit content on them or assume everyone wants a lesson full of swearing. But we shouldn’t quietly turn our own discomfort into a rule about what they’re allowed to learn. If we advertise real-life English, answering uncomfortable questions is part of the responsibility we’re taking on.

Yes, even those words

I built a lesson around compound words and expressions, including “inside job,” “put-up job,” “hand job,” “rim job,” “hardcore” and “softcore porn.” (See the recommended lesson plans below)

Quite a range. Definitely not interchangeable.

The point wasn’t to see how many dirty expressions I could squeeze onto a worksheet. It was to bring language students might encounter in films, comments and songs into a space where they could ask questions about it.

What does it mean? Is it vulgar? Is it insulting? Would people actually say it aloud, or is it something you’re more likely to read online?

These are perfectly reasonable language questions.

Not every expression belongs in every lesson. I’d explain the content beforehand and give adults a genuine option to skip it. But “this isn’t relevant to these students” is a better reason to leave something out than “I’d rather they Googled it.”

Teach the language and the consequences

Getting students acquainted with this side of English doesn’t mean encouraging them to use it.

Take these three responses:

  • “I’m not convinced.”

  • “That’s not true.”

  • “That’s bullshit.”

Put them into the same workplace conversation. Ask students what changes. Then change the relationship: a manager and an employee, two close colleagues, two friends in a pub.

Now there’s something worth teaching.

You can discuss how strongly the speaker rejects the idea, whether they’re challenging someone personally, and what saying it might cost them. Students can practice the neutral alternative while still understanding the stronger one.

A dictionary definition won’t do all that work. Neither will “It’s a bad word. Don’t say it.”

And if I’m unsure about an expression myself, I can say so and check. Taking responsibility doesn’t require knowing every dirty joke on the internet.

Don’t leave them at the classroom door

Textbook English is English. It’s just a selection of it.

My problem is with tutors who sell that selection as preparation for real life, then leave students unsupported when real life gets awkward.
Take my honeymoon trip to Tunisia, for example. The very first day we left the hotel premises, we were cornered by kids selling flowers. I politely brushed one off, and in response, I got a "Fuck you!" My point is that even kids know what real English sounds like, yet as tutors, we throw a towel over it and pretend it doesn't exist.

For adults learning through media and conversations, some guidance on slang, swearing and taboo language should be a normal part of that preparation. How much depends on the student. Pretending none of it matters shouldn’t be the default.

Keep the textbook. Recommend the film.

But when your student comes back and asks, “What the fuck does this mean?” - well, there’s your teaching opportunity.
