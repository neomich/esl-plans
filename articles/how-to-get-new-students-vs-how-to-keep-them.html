How to Get New Students vs How to Keep Them

20 November 2026 · by Alex · ESL-plans Teacher's Corner

Here’s a post you’ve probably seen in a teachers’ group. Or written yourself: Dear English teachers! Please help! I post my ads everywhere every day to no avail! No new students at all! How do you get new students??? I need a professional advice!🙏🥺

Fair question. Bills don’t pay themselves, and an empty calendar is stressful. But I rarely see the other question:

How do you keep the students you already have?

In fifteen years of teaching, that question has done more for my schedule than posting ads everywhere. Students stay, come back after breaks, recommend you to colleagues, and eventually send their partners your way.

None of this happens automatically because you’re a qualified teacher with a nice worksheet to teach. People need a reason to keep choosing you.

Have a relationship, not just a booking

If all your communication consists of lesson links, homework and payment reminders, you’re missing quite a lot.

A student mentioned a disastrous job interview? Ask how the next one went. You discussed office surveillance last week? Send them the ridiculous LinkedIn post you’ve just found about monitoring employees’ toilet breaks.

“Remember our conversation? Apparently, it gets worse.”

You’re giving the language another outing and keeping a conversation alive between lessons. More importantly, you reminded them what you talked about.

I often connect with students on Instagram. We react to stories, exchange posts, occasionally take the piss out of something. Nothing forced. If someone prefers to keep their account private, that’s fine too.

You don’t need to manufacture friendship or become available around the clock. But you do need to be interested in the person who keeps showing up.

Let tired students take a break

Some of my students are on-and-off students. They study, disappear for a while, then come back.

I’m fine with that. Sometimes I’m the one who suggests it.

When someone arrives exhausted, apologizes for the homework again, and looks like your lesson is the final administrative task standing between them and bed, consider whether another motivational speech is really what they need.

“Listen, you’ve got a lot going on. Shall we take a couple of weeks off?”

Yes, that means losing some money now. I have bills too. But I’d rather have that conversation than watch someone gradually associate English lessons with guilt.

We can agree on when I’ll check in, so they don’t have to make an awkward comeback three months later.

If you treat every pause as a lost customer, you may make it unnecessarily difficult for people to return. Some of my longest teaching relationships have included quite a few pauses.

Be honest about what you can do

I can help a student prepare for a presentation. I can notice the language problems they keep running into. I can give them practice, feedback, structure and a reason to speak English regularly.

I cannot promise that they’ll become fluent by June because they bought twenty lessons.

And I don’t want to spend those twenty lessons defending a promise I had no business making.

Tell people what working with you involves. Ask what they can realistically manage. If their goal and their available time don’t match, discuss it before you take their money.

You’re going to spend a lot of time together. It helps if neither of you is waiting for a miracle.

Have an opinion. They’ve already got a mirror.

I don’t want my lessons to feel like talking to a dashboard ornament.

“Yes, absolutely.”
“That’s a very interesting opinion.”
“I completely agree.”

Do you, though?

If a student says all meetings are pointless, I might ask how their team would function without any. If they think working from home suits everyone, I might disagree.

Now they have something to respond to. They need to explain, defend, reconsider, find a better example. There’s a reason to use the language.

Obviously, don’t turn every conversation into a fight. And remember that some people find disagreeing with their teacher uncomfortable. Give them room to push back without feeling they’re being tested.

But please, be someone. A student shouldn’t have to supply both sides of the conversation.

Listen for next week’s lesson

“What topics are you interested in?” often produces a painfully unhelpful answer.

“Travel. Films. Business.”

Listen to what comes up when they’re not trying to answer a teacher’s question.

Their boss sends messages at midnight. Their teenager won’t put the phone down. They’re tired of being told to turn every hobby into a side hustle.

There’s your lesson material.

You don’t need to interrogate anyone or extract a confession. People tell you plenty when they feel comfortable and you give them time. Then, next week: “You mentioned something the other day. I found this and thought we should talk about it.” That moment matters. Their throwaway comment wasn’t throwaway to you. You listened, remembered, and did something with it.

Give them a nudge when they need one

Sometimes a student is exhausted. Sometimes they just couldn’t be bothered. There’s a difference, although you won’t always get it right. With some students, I can send: “Sorry, I may have missed your homework message. Could you please send it again?😜”

With others, a straightforward “I hear a HW crying somewhere... all alone, calling your name!?” works better.

The relationship determines the message. A joke that works with someone you’ve taught for three years might sound obnoxious to someone you met last Tuesday. It’s worth asking early on whether they want reminders and how much pushing they find useful. Then you’re not guessing.

I don’t chase every unfinished exercise. But if we’ve agreed on something important, I won’t always politely pretend I’ve forgotten it too.

Make room for a good laugh

I want us to laugh in lessons. I enjoy teaching more that way, and I like working with people who can laugh with me. A ridiculous example, an unfortunate choice of words, a shared joke that resurfaces six months later - these things become part of your history together.

Humor can also go wrong. You misread the mood. A sarcastic comment sounds harsher than intended. Your student goes quiet. Don’t explain why they should have found it funny.

“Sorry, that came out wrong” is usually a better start. Drop the joke, change direction, and remember the boundary next time. If you’re unsure whether you upset them, ask privately rather than making them reassure you during the lesson.

You’re allowed to be funny. You’re also responsible for noticing when you weren’t.

Remember what they couldn’t do before

There’s another thing I try to keep track of: the problems students stop having.

They used to rehearse every sentence before speaking. Now they interrupt me to disagree. They used to avoid phone calls. Last week, they dealt with a delivery problem in English without thinking much about it.

Point that out. Be specific.

People can forget what used to be difficult, especially when they’re busy worrying about everything they still can’t do. Your memory can help them see whether the lessons are actually doing something.

And occasionally, ask what they’d change about the lessons themselves. “Would you like me to correct you more often?” is easier to answer honestly than “Are you happy with everything?”

Then do something with the answer.

Where the new students come from

Over fifteen years, these relationships have brought me recommendations, returning students, and messages beginning with “You teach a friend of mine…”

It’s not a quick fix. A happy student won’t necessarily refer anyone, and word of mouth may move considerably slower than your rent deadline. Sometimes you still need the ads.

But I’d rather advertise while knowing that the people who arrive have a good reason to stay.

So yes, work out how to get new students.

And when the next one books, give the other question some proper attention:

How do I keep this person - before I start looking for another one?
